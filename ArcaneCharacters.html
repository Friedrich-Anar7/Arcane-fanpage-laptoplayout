Childhood

I grew up with Vi, Mylo, and Claggor under Vander's roof.

We didn't have much, but we had each other, and for a while, that was enough.

Vi was always the one who believed she could handle anything, and I wanted to be just like her. I was smaller, slower, and better with a wrench than a fist. I built little things, took them apart, and put them back together when they broke.

Then came the bridge. After that, everything changed. Vander gave us a home, but I never stopped wondering if I was strong enough to belong in it.

Motivation

I wanted to prove that I wasn't Powder anymore. Powder was the girl who always got left behind, the one who needed Vi to come back and tell her everything was going to be okay. I didn't want to be that girl again.

I wanted to be useful. Dangerous, even. Someone people couldn't throw away so easily. Silco gave me a place where being broken didn't make me worthless. He believed in what I could become, and I held on to that harder than I should have.

But underneath all the noise, the guns, and the explosions, I was still chasing the same thing I'd wanted as a kid: to know that someone would choose me and stay.

Journey

I started as Powder, running through the Lanes with Vi, Mylo, and Claggor, thinking we could take on anything.

Then one night changed everything. I lost Vander, lost my home, and became someone I barely recognized.

Silco took me in, and the years that followed shaped me into Jinx. I built, fought, broke things, and learned to survive on my own terms.

But when Vi returned, the life I'd built began colliding with the life I'd buried. I spent years trying to outrun Powder, only to realize she had never really left.

My journey was never about becoming someone else. It was about figuring out who I was when I couldn't go back.

Sacrifice

I lost more than people.

I lost the life I thought I'd have with my sister. I lost Vander, Mylo, Claggor, and the girl I used to be.

Every time I tried to build something new, part of the old world came back to collect its price. I gave up trust before I could be abandoned again.

I gave up the chance to be Powder because being Powder hurt too much. And even when I found people who cared about me, I kept pushing them away before they could become another thing I could lose.

Maybe that's the worst part. Sometimes surviving means becoming someone you never meant to be.

Philosophy

Maybe there isn't a Powder and a Jinx.

Maybe there never was.

People love putting names on things they don't understand. Good. Bad. Broken. Dangerous. I stopped trying to fit into those little boxes a long time ago.

People leave. That's what they do. So I learned not to need them. I learned to build things that don't walk away, and to laugh when everything falls apart.

Maybe that's why I like explosions. For one perfect second, everything is honest. No pretending. No past. No one telling me who I'm supposed to be.

I'm Jinx. That's enough.

Legacy

I don't know what people will remember when I'm gone.

Maybe the explosions. Maybe the blue smoke. Maybe the girl everyone was afraid to call by her real name.

But I know I changed things. I left marks on Piltover, on Zaun, and on everyone who crossed my path. Some of them will hate me. Some might understand me. That's fine.

I stopped trying to control what people think of me a long time ago. I spent my whole life trying to prove I wasn't the girl who broke everything. Maybe I was. Maybe that's not the whole story.

I built things. I protected people. I destroyed things. I loved people. That's my legacy. All of it.

I never believed Zaun needed saving; it needed someone willing to believe in it completely.

Legacy

I wanted Zaun to stand on its own, and in the end it did—not exactly as I imagined, but close enough to prove the dream wasn't impossible.

I left behind a city that no longer had to ask Piltover for permission, and I left behind scars, enemies, and people who paid for my choices.

Perhaps that is the truth of any revolution: you can change the world and still leave it damaged.

They may remember me as a monster or as the man who gave Zaun its freedom. I can live with either. What matters is that Zaun is finally its own.

Not because I was afraid to fight, but because I knew exactly what another fight would cost. If I have any rule, it's this: the children shouldn't have to pay for the mistakes of the people who came before them.

Legacy

I gave a few children a home. I taught them to look after each other, to stand together, and to believe they could become something different from the world around them.

Maybe that's the legacy I wanted. Not a city that remembers my name. Just people who remember how to protect each other.

Origins

I was a scientist long before Zaun became my laboratory. Knowledge was never a hobby to me. It was a necessity.

The natural world is full of questions, and I have never understood the desire to leave them unanswered simply because the answers are unpleasant.

Piltover offered knowledge, but only within boundaries it considered acceptable. Zaun offered something different: freedom to pursue what others were unwilling to touch.

So I came to the Undercity. Not for its politics. Not for its people. For the work.

Motivation

I don't pursue knowledge for recognition. Recognition is irrelevant.

What matters is the question, and whether I am willing to follow it to its conclusion.

I have spent years trying to overcome the limits imposed by nature. Disease. Weakness. Mortality.

Every discovery opens another door, and behind that door is another problem demanding an answer.

People call some questions dangerous because they fear what the answers might require. I have never found fear to be a particularly useful scientific principle.

Journey

I left Piltover behind when its scholars decided that knowledge was only valuable when it remained comfortable. Zaun offered no such restrictions. There, I could pursue my work without asking permission.

My research took me into places most would consider unacceptable. Shimmer was only one step along the way.

Each experiment revealed something new, and each discovery demanded that I go further. My work has taken many forms, and many lives have been affected by it.

That is the nature of progress. It rarely moves in a straight line, and it is rarely clean. I simply continued where the evidence led.

Sacrifice

I have never believed that progress comes without a price. Every experiment demands something. Time. Resources. Certainty. Sometimes more.

I lost people I cared for because I chose the work over everything else.

I watched my own daughter suffer, and still I continued searching for a way to save her. That is perhaps the greatest sacrifice I have made, not because I was willing to lose her, but because I was unwilling to accept that she could be lost.

If knowledge demands a price, I will pay it. The question is only whether the result is worth what remains.

Philosophy

I do not believe knowledge should be restrained by discomfort. Nature does not concern itself with morality. It simply operates according to its laws, and our task is to understand them.

People fear what they cannot understand. They call an experiment cruel when they see the suffering, but rarely ask what might be learned from it.

Perhaps that makes me ruthless. Perhaps it makes me practical. I have never confused being compassionate with being useful.

If a discovery can change what is possible, then the discomfort required to reach it is not an argument against pursuing it. Progress does not ask whether we are ready. It simply asks whether we are willing to continue.

Legacy

I never cared whether my name was remembered. Names fade. The work remains.

The things I created have already changed the world, whether for better or worse. Shimmer, my research, the lives altered by my experiments, all of it became part of something larger than myself.

Perhaps that is the only legacy worth having. Not being remembered as a man. Being remembered as the one who refused to accept that anything was impossible.

Viktor

Origins

I was born in Zaun, where sickness was not an exception but a part of life. My own body was fragile, and I learned early what it meant to live with limitations that other people could simply ignore. I could have accepted them as fate. Instead, I became obsessed with understanding why things were the way they were.

Science gave me something that Zaun rarely offered: a way to look at suffering without simply accepting it. Machines could be repaired. Diseases could be studied. Systems could be improved. If something was flawed, perhaps it did not have to remain flawed.

My work eventually brought me to Piltover, where I found opportunities that would have been almost impossible to imagine in the Undercity. But arriving there did not make me forget where I came from. Piltover's progress was extraordinary, yet much of that progress existed far beyond the people who needed it most.

I began to believe that knowledge should not belong only to those fortunate enough to afford it. If science could change the world, then it should be used to change the lives of the people who had been left behind by it.

Motivation

I have never been motivated by the desire to become powerful. Power, on its own, solves very little. What matters to me is the ability to overcome the limitations that dictate who gets to live a better life and who is simply expected to endure.

My own illness made that question personal. I knew what it meant to feel that your body was working against you, to understand that there were things you could not do no matter how desperately you wanted to. I refused to believe that those limitations should define the boundaries of a person's life.

That belief became the foundation of my work. Hextech was not merely a scientific achievement to me. It was proof that humanity could reach beyond what it had previously considered impossible. Science could give ordinary people access to extraordinary possibilities.

But there was another part of my motivation that I understood only later. I wasn't simply trying to help humanity overcome suffering. I was trying to overcome my own fear of weakness, decay, and death. The more my body failed me, the harder I pushed toward a solution that could make weakness irrelevant.

Perhaps that is where ambition becomes dangerous. When the thing you are trying to defeat is also the thing you fear most, it becomes difficult to know when the pursuit of progress has stopped being about helping others and started becoming about escaping yourself.

Journey

I began my journey believing that science could solve the problems that had shaped my life. With Jayce, I helped turn Hextech from an impossible idea into something real. For the first time, I saw a future where technology could exist beyond Piltover's privileged circles and reach people who had spent their lives without access to it.

But progress rarely moves in a straight line. As my illness worsened, the distance between what I wanted to achieve and the time I had left became impossible to ignore. I became more willing to take risks, more willing to question limits that once seemed necessary, and less willing to accept that my body should determine what I could become.

The Hexcore changed the direction of that journey. What began as an attempt to understand and control its power became something far more personal. It offered the possibility of transformation, but every step toward that possibility demanded that I give up something of myself.

Eventually, my journey stopped being only about creating better technology. It became a question of what humanity itself could become if its weaknesses were no longer permanent.

Glorious Evolution

The Glorious Evolution began as an idea: humanity did not have to remain trapped by the limitations of the flesh. Disease, weakness, disability, and even the boundaries of the human body could be challenged through science.

I did not see evolution as something that simply happened to us. I believed we could take control of it. We could improve ourselves deliberately rather than waiting for nature to decide what we were capable of becoming.

The more deeply I connected with the Hexcore, the more that belief became tangible. Transformation was no longer theoretical. I could see what might be possible if technology and humanity were no longer separate things.

But there was a price hidden inside that vision. Improvement requires deciding what counts as an imperfection. Once I began viewing the human body as something that could be redesigned, it became easier to see suffering as a flaw to be eliminated rather than an experience to be understood.

The Glorious Evolution was never simply about replacing humanity with something new. It was born from the desire to free humanity from everything that limits it. The tragedy is that, in trying to remove those limitations, I risked losing sight of what made humanity worth saving in the first place.

Philosophy

I have never believed that suffering becomes meaningful simply because it is inevitable. If something can be changed, then why should we accept it merely because generations before us had no choice?

Humanity has always evolved by overcoming its limitations. We built machines to extend our strength, medicine to overcome disease, and technology to accomplish what the human body alone could not. I saw no reason that process should stop at the boundaries of our own biology.

But progress requires more than the ability to change things. It requires knowing what should remain unchanged. That distinction became harder for me as I continued my work. When you spend your life studying weakness, it becomes tempting to see every limitation as a problem waiting to be solved.

Perhaps the greatest question is not whether humanity can become something greater. It is whether, in becoming greater, we can still recognize ourselves.

I wanted to free people from suffering. I wanted to give them possibilities they had never possessed. But somewhere along the way, I had to confront an uncomfortable truth: removing every weakness does not necessarily create a better human being. Sometimes our limitations are not merely obstacles. Sometimes they are part of what makes us human.

Legacy

I never wanted to be remembered simply as the scientist who helped create Hextech. Scientific discoveries are only the beginning. What matters is what people choose to do with them after their creators are gone.

My work gave humanity possibilities that once seemed impossible. It also forced people to confront questions that science alone cannot answer. How much should we change ourselves? When does progress become something dangerous? And if we have the ability to eliminate human weakness, should we?

Perhaps that is what I leave behind more than anything else: not an answer, but a question. I wanted to create a future where no one would have to suffer because of limitations they never chose.

Whether that future would truly be better was something I could never completely prove. But I believe the attempt to understand, improve, and move beyond what we know is part of what makes us human.

If my work has a legacy, I hope it is not simply the technology I created. I hope it is the courage to question the limits we inherit, while remembering that progress without humanity is only another form of failure.

Her body continued to fail, and Singed continued to search for answers in places where science became less about understanding life and more about refusing to accept its limits.

Eventually, Orianna's human body could no longer carry her forward. Piece by piece, she was rebuilt, her fragile existence preserved through machinery and experimentation. What began as a father's attempt to save his daughter slowly became something far more complicated: a question of how much of a person can be changed before saving them becomes creating someone new.

By the time Orianna emerges at the end of the story, she is no longer the sickly girl Singed once fought to protect. She is something new, standing at the boundary between human and machine, carrying the quiet history of everything that was sacrificed to keep her alive.

Her story is not simply about becoming mechanical. It is about the lengths love can travel when it refuses to accept goodbye.

Singed

To the world, Singed is a man who treats life as a problem waiting to be solved. To Orianna, he was her father, and that difference changes everything.

When illness began taking his daughter from him, Singed refused to accept the ending nature had written. He searched through science, medicine, and eventually darker forms of experimentation, following every possibility that might give Orianna another day.

But his love became tangled with obsession. The more of Orianna he lost, the further he was willing to go to preserve what remained. The experiments that horrify everyone else were, to him, the desperate work of a father standing between his daughter and death.

Their story is one of *Arcane's* cruelest contradictions. Singed does terrible things in the name of saving life, yet the reason he keeps reaching beyond every moral boundary is not power, glory, or conquest. It is Orianna.

He could accept almost anything about himself. What he could not accept was losing her.

Transformation

Orianna's transformation was never a single moment. It was a slow surrender, each change made in the hope that the next one would be the last.

As her human body failed, Singed replaced what could no longer endure. Flesh gave way to machinery, weakness to precision, until the fragile girl he had once carried through illness could survive in a body built rather than born.

Yet the strange thing about Orianna is not that she became mechanical. It is that something of her remained. Her movements, her presence, the quiet humanity behind her eyes, all suggest that the machine did not simply replace the girl. It became the vessel that carried her forward.

By the end, Orianna stands as something neither entirely human nor entirely machine. She is the result of a father's refusal to let death have the final word, and the living proof that saving someone can sometimes mean changing them beyond recognition.

She did not simply survive the loss of her old body. She became the answer to the question Singed had spent his life asking: how far can life be changed before it becomes something new?

The Black Rose

Long before Piltover knew the name Hextech, before Zaun became a battlefield of shimmer and steel, another power had already learned how to move through Noxus without ever being seen. It was called the Black Rose.

Hidden beneath the empire's wars and ambitions, the Black Rose was a secret network built around influence, manipulation, and ancient power. It did not need armies marching beneath its banner. At its heart stood LeBlanc, a woman who had survived long enough to understand that the greatest power is not always the kind that stands in the light. She became one of the Rose's most important figures, shaping events from behind masks while allowing others to believe they were acting on their own.

Her reach stretches far beyond what Arcane openly reveals. The Black Rose is older than the conflicts surrounding Piltover and Zaun, and its interests extend into places where magic, politics, and ancient forces begin to overlap.

That is what makes LeBlanc so difficult to find in the story. She does not arrive like a storm. She arrives like a shadow that was already there.

The Masks

With LeBlanc, a face is rarely just a face. She has spent centuries turning identity into a weapon, appearing as the people others trust while keeping the woman beneath the mask safely out of reach.

She can take another form, borrow another voice, and step into another person's life as though it were a garment. The deception is so complete that even those closest to her may never know whether they have met LeBlanc herself or merely another reflection.

But the masks are more than simple illusions. They allow her to move through the world without becoming part of it, to influence kings, enemies, and allies while leaving almost nothing behind. Every appearance can conceal another intention, and every truth can be wrapped inside another lie.

This makes LeBlanc almost impossible to understand from the outside. The woman seen in one moment may disappear in the next, leaving only the question of whether she was ever truly there.

For LeBlanc, the mask is not something she wears to hide herself. It is how she chooses to exist.

The Deceiver

LeBlanc's greatest weapon is not a blade, an army, or even magic. It is uncertainty.

She rarely gives the world the truth when a convincing lie will serve her better. A face can become a disguise, a conversation can become a trap, and an ally can become a pawn without ever realizing the game has begun.

She does not simply deceive people. She makes them deceive themselves. By giving others just enough truth to believe the rest, she can turn their fears, desires, and ambitions against them. The victim often believes the choice was their own long before they realize someone else placed it in front of them.

That is the essence of the Deceiver. LeBlanc does not need to control every move. She only needs to make the board look different enough that everyone else moves exactly where she wants them to.

And somewhere behind every face she wears, the real LeBlanc remains hidden.

Significance

LeBlanc is a glimpse of a world much larger than the one Arcane places before us. Piltover and Zaun may seem like the heart of the story, but beyond their streets lie powers that have been moving pieces long before Jayce ever touched Hextech.

Her presence connects Arcane to the older, darker forces of Runeterra. The Black Rose is not simply another political faction. It is a shadow beneath Noxus, carrying secrets, magic, and ambitions that reach far beyond a single war or a single generation.

What makes LeBlanc significant is precisely how little she reveals. She does not need to stand at the center of the story to influence it. Her appearance suggests that the world has deeper layers, and that some of its most dangerous players have not yet stepped fully into the light.

She leaves behind more questions than answers. Who was truly speaking? Which face was hers? How long had she been watching? In Arcane, sometimes the most important presence is the one you cannot quite prove was there.

The Mage

He arrives without a name, without a history, and without explanation. A hood hides his face, but the magic surrounding him speaks louder than any introduction could. He appears in the moments when Jayce's life hangs by a thread, stepping from the impossible as though he has already walked the path ahead.

To Jayce, he is a savior. To the story, he is something far stranger. He carries knowledge that should not yet exist, moves through places ordinary men could never reach, and seems to understand the consequences of events long before they unfold.

The mage does not seek recognition. He gives Jayce the means to survive, leaves behind the knowledge that will eventually become Hextech, and disappears back into the shadows. His intervention is small in the moment, almost insignificant against the scale of what follows.

Yet that single act becomes the first thread in a much larger pattern. The man who saves Jayce is not simply witnessing the future. He is already part of it.

And beneath the hood lies a truth that makes the mystery even deeper: **he is Viktor, reaching back through time to save the world that created him.**

The Choices

The mage does not simply appear in Jayce's life. He chooses him.

He finds Jayce at the moment when a young inventor is about to lose everything, when science has reached its limit and magic offers no promise of salvation. Instead of letting the moment pass, he intervenes. He gives Jayce the rune, the knowledge, and the smallest glimpse of a power that will eventually reshape the world.

But his choices do not end there. Again and again, the path bends toward Jayce. The mage appears when the future demands another hand upon the scale, guiding events without ever standing openly within them.

Every intervention carries a cost. By saving Jayce, he creates Hextech. By creating Hextech, he sets Viktor on the path toward the Glorious Evolution. And by allowing that path to unfold, he eventually creates the very future from which he came.

His choices are therefore not simple acts of mercy. They are pieces placed carefully into a game whose board he has already seen.

He saves Jayce because, somewhere in the future, he already knows what Jayce must become.

The Cycle

The mage's story is not a straight road. It is a circle.

Viktor reaches the end of his journey and sees a future where his pursuit of perfection has become a catastrophe. The world he tried to save is dying beneath the weight of his own creation. Yet instead of destroying everything, he reaches backward through time, becoming the mysterious mage who once saved Jayce.

He gives Jayce the rune that begins the path toward Hextech. Jayce and Viktor discover its power, Hextech changes the world, and Viktor's journey eventually leads him to the very future from which he intervened.

Then the circle closes. The future creates the past, and the past creates the future. There is no clear beginning, no first hand that placed the stone into Jayce's hands. Only the endless movement of cause becoming consequence, and consequence becoming cause.

The mage does not escape the cycle. He becomes part of it, repeating the same intervention across time in the hope that, one day, the story will finally end differently.

The Deeds

He is remembered not for what he says, but for what he leaves behind. He saves Jayce from the cold, gives him the rune that will become the key to Hextech, and vanishes before the boy can understand what has happened. Years later, the gift has become a force capable of changing cities, armies, and the course of history itself.

He returns again when Jayce stands at another crossroads, guiding him toward the choice that will eventually break the cycle. His hand is rarely visible, yet his influence can be felt in the moments where everything might have gone another way.

Beyond Jayce, his greatest deed is the one hidden beneath all the others: he reaches backward through time because he has seen what Viktor's evolution will become. He does not simply try to save a person. He tries to save a world from the future he himself helped create.

Every act carries the same strange contradiction. He creates the path he later tries to escape, leaving behind the very pieces that will eventually lead him back to the beginning.

The mage's deeds are therefore not monuments. They are threads, each one pulling the world closer to the moment when the circle might finally break.

Significance

The Hooded Mage is one of Arcane's deepest mysteries because he turns the story itself into a question of cause and consequence.

He reveals that time in Arcane is not simply a road leading from past to future. It can fold back upon itself, carrying knowledge, regret, and hope into the moments that created them. The hand that saves Jayce belongs to the future, yet that future could never exist without the act that came before it.

His existence also changes how Viktor's journey is understood. The man who once sought perfection eventually becomes the one who learns its cost. Having seen where his vision leads, he reaches back through time not to erase his existence, but to give the people who created him another chance.

That makes the mage more than a mysterious figure beneath a hood. He is the embodiment of Arcane's central tension: **whether a life is defined by what it becomes, or by the choices made along the way.

He stands at both ends of the story, watching the same spark ignite again and again, waiting for someone to finally choose a different path.

The Evolution

It began with a question Viktor could never leave alone: what if humanity did not have to remain as it was?

The answer was not found in comfort, nor in the fragile machinery of Hextech. It emerged through the Arcane itself, through a transformation that slowly stripped away the weaknesses Viktor had spent his life trying to overcome.

What began as an attempt to heal became something far greater. Flesh gave way to something more enduring. Individual minds became connected. Pain, fear, illness, and the divisions that had followed humanity for generations began to look like flaws that could simply be removed.

Viktor called it evolution. To those who stood outside it, it looked like something else entirely: humanity being perfected until there was almost nothing human left to recognize.

The Glorious Evolved was the final shape of that idea. Not merely a stronger Viktor, but a vision made flesh, where the individual would disappear into something greater, cleaner, and seemingly without suffering.

And that was the beauty and horror of it. Viktor had finally found a way to end human weakness. He had only forgotten that weakness was also where humanity lived.

The Army

The Glorious Evolved was no longer one man. It became a collective.

Those who entered Viktor's evolution were stripped of the boundaries that had separated one mind from another. Their bodies became vessels of the same golden power, their thoughts joined beneath a single vision, and their individuality slowly gave way to something greater than themselves.

They moved not as soldiers following a commander, but as parts of one living consciousness. There was no fear of death, no hunger for power, no need for conflict between individuals. The weaknesses that had divided humanity were replaced by perfect unity.

To Viktor, this was freedom from everything that had made humanity suffer. To those watching from outside, it was something far more unsettling: an army where every soldier had surrendered the thing that made them an individual.

The Glorious Evolved promised a world without division. Its price was the self.

The Vision

Viktor's vision was never simply to make humanity stronger. He wanted to free it from the suffering that had followed it since the beginning.

Pain, illness, fear, conflict, and the loneliness of the individual all appeared to him as flaws in a system that had never been perfected. If those flaws could be removed, then perhaps humanity could finally become something whole.

The Glorious Evolution was his answer. A world where no one would have to suffer because no one would have to remain bound to the fragile limits of flesh and individual will. Every mind could become part of something greater, every life connected to the same purpose.

But Viktor's vision carried a quiet contradiction. In trying to create a world without suffering, he began removing the very differences, desires, and imperfections that gave people their humanity.

He wanted to end the loneliness of being human. In doing so, he came dangerously close to ending the human itself.

The End

Viktor's vision promised an end to suffering, but every promise made to humanity carries a price. The Glorious Evolution did not simply change bodies. It changed what it meant to be a person.

As more people became part of the evolution, individuality began to disappear. The pain Viktor hated was gone, but so were the differences that made each person themselves. There was peace, but it was the stillness of a world where no one could truly stand apart.

The greatest consequence was not the army itself. It was Viktor's realization that he had mistaken the absence of suffering for the presence of happiness. He had built a world where nothing could hurt, only to discover that nothing could truly live as it once had either.

The Glorious Evolved became the final mirror held before Viktor. He saw his dream made real, perfect and terrifying, and finally understood what it had cost.

In trying to save humanity from its imperfections, Viktor had nearly erased the very imperfections that made humanity worth saving.

Jinx & Vi

Family — File Case

Jinx & Vi

Sisters

Before either name meant what it means now, they were just two kids sharing one mattress in the same cramped room above the Lanes. Vi was six years older and acted like it — she threw the punches so Powder wouldn't have to, ate less so there'd be more for her, slept closest to the door on the nights Vander's rules weren't enough to keep the Lanes out. Powder noticed things Vi never had the patience for: which wire was live, which crate would hold your weight, which adult in a room was worth watching and which one wasn't. Neither of them called it a system. It just worked, the way things a kid builds out of necessity tend to.

It wasn't an equal trade — Vi gave protection, Powder gave a kind of attentiveness that read almost like magic to everyone around her — but it never needed to be equal. That's what made it easy. Before there was anything to prove or defend, there was just this: one sister keeping watch, the other one figuring out the world's smaller mechanics, both of them assuming, without ever saying it out loud, that the other one would still be there tomorrow.

Bound

Being sisters was where it started. What made them inseparable was the work. On every job, Vi took the front — the fight, the risk, whatever stood between Powder and getting hurt. Powder took the part Vi had no patience for: rigging the device, reading the room, finding the way in nobody else saw. Neither role worked without the other, and over enough jobs pulled the same way, that stopped being a plan and started being just how the two of them operated.

It showed most in the small habits neither of them had to think about — Vi never checking whether Powder was behind her, because she already knew; Powder never hesitating before handing over something that could blow up in the wrong hands, because it wouldn't occur to her that Vi's hands were the wrong ones. That's what "bound" meant here: not a promise either of them made out loud, just certainty neither had ever had to test — until the one night that certainty was the thing on the line.

The Shattering

The job that was supposed to be like every other job wasn't. Powder's device went off when it shouldn't have, and in the chaos that followed, Vander was killed and Vi was taken by the enforcers — arrested and dragged away in the same night Powder was left standing alone in the wreckage of everything the two of them had built together. There was no argument, no goodbye, no moment either of them got to choose. One version of their partnership ended mid-motion, the way accidents end things, not the way stories usually let them.

What Powder was left with wasn't just grief — it was the specific, corrosive belief that she had caused it. Vi wasn't there to tell her otherwise, and the person who was — Silco — had every reason to let that belief take root instead of correcting it. The girl who used to hand Vi anything without hesitation now had no one to hand anything to. That absence is where Jinx starts.

The Search for Powder

Years later, out of custody and back in Zaun, Vi doesn't look for a rival gang leader or a wanted criminal — she looks for her sister, using the only name she's ever known her by. That gap is the whole tragedy of this chapter: everyone around Vi already knows Powder as Jinx, and Vi has to catch up to a version of events she wasn't present for. She asks about Powder in rooms where the answer is Jinx, and gets treated like she's either naive or dangerous for not already knowing it.

The trail she follows keeps leading back to one name, one reputation: a feared, erratic figure operating under Silco's protection, someone Zaun has learned to fear rather than pity. Piece by piece, Vi has to accept that the stories aren't about a different person who happens to share her sister's face — they're about Powder, changed by four years Vi wasn't there for. Reconciling the two is the hardest part of the search, harder than finding her at all.

When she finally finds her, the reunion isn't the relief it should be. Jinx meets her armed, guarded, and unconvinced that "Vi's sister" is still a role she fits. Vi keeps reaching for Powder anyway, even as the person in front of her keeps making it clear that reaching might not be enough anymore.

What Endured?

Not the old certainty — that much is gone, and neither of them pretends otherwise. What's left is smaller and harder to name: Jinx still knows exactly who Vi is to her, even when she's pointing a weapon in her direction. Vi still won't let go, even when letting go would be easier. That's not the bond they had as kids. It's what's left of it after everything that broke it.

It shows up less in what either of them says and more in what Jinx does when it counts. For all her instability, all the ways she pushes Vi away or turns a gun on the people around her, when a moment comes down to a choice between Vi and everything else, Jinx keeps choosing Vi. That's not forgiveness, and it's not the relationship healing — it's just proof the old wiring never fully disconnected, even under everything built on top of it since.

Vi & Vander

Family — File Case

Vi & Vander

What She Understood

Vi was old enough to know what was actually happening when Vander took them in — not just that they'd been given somewhere to stay, but that a man with his own rules and his own reasons had decided, deliberately, to make room for them. She understood his position in Zaun well enough to know that choice cost him something, in a way Powder, younger and further from the reasoning behind it, simply couldn't yet. That understanding didn't stay one-directional for long — Vi started paying attention to what his protection required of him, the risks he carried and the trust he was extending, and began treating his household as something worth holding up her end of, rather than something owed to her.

That was the difference between how the two sisters entered his life. Powder inherited a home. Vi recognized one being built, in real time, around a decision Vander didn't have to make. Being part of his family meant she'd been given something. It also meant she now had something worth protecting — a distinction that would come to define most of what came after.

Raised to Endure

Vander didn't simply tell Vi to be strong — he built situations where her strength was needed, and let her step into them. Restraint, when to fight and when not to, how to stand between danger and the people behind her: these weren't lessons delivered as speeches. They were expectations she grew into because he treated her as someone capable of meeting them, again and again, until meeting them stopped feeling like something she was being tested on and started feeling like who she was.

That wasn't a judgment on Powder, just a difference in what age made possible. Vi was old enough to be relied on, so Vander relied on her — not as a lesser form of affection than the patience he showed her sister, but as its own kind of trust, suited to what Vi could actually carry. He wasn't preparing her to suffer. He was preparing her to be someone others could stand behind.

The cost of that preparation was quiet enough that it probably didn't look like a cost at all, not at the time. The same lessons that made her someone Vander could hand real weight to also taught her, without either of them meaning it, to carry things herself rather than hand them to someone else. Being dependable and being alone with a problem started, slowly, to feel like the same skill.

The Weight

Being the one Vander could count on stopped being just strength and started being responsibility — looking after Powder, making the call when something went wrong, stepping forward before anyone asked her to. Vi didn't resent it. She wanted to live up to what he'd taught her, and every time she did, the expectation that she'd keep doing it settled a little deeper.

That's where trust like Vander's has a cost nobody names out loud: Vi came to believe that carrying things herself wasn't just something she was good at, it was what she was for. Being dependable had started as something he built in her. By the time it mattered most, it had become something she expected of herself, whether or not anyone was actually asking.

None of this makes Vander responsible for what it cost her — he never meant to teach her that she had to shoulder everything alone, and most of what he gave her was exactly what she needed to survive Zaun. He just never got the chance to teach her the opposite lesson, the one about when it's alright to put something down, before the night arrived that asked more of her than being strong could answer.

The Last Night

Vi went looking for Vander the moment things started going wrong — her instinct wasn't to run, it was to fix it: get him out, keep Powder safe, hold the situation together the way she'd been trained to, the instinct Vander built into her precisely for nights like this one. It wasn't enough. She found him at the center of exactly the danger she'd been trying to pull him out of, and there was nothing left to do that would undo it — everything she'd been raised to be colliding directly with a situation that couldn't be fixed by stepping forward at all.

Then came the enforcers, and Vi was taken before she had the chance to process what she'd just lost, let alone find Powder in the chaos that followed. She didn't choose to leave her sister behind. She simply ran out of time to do anything else — and unlike Powder, she wouldn't even learn what happened next for years.

What Was Taken

Vander was the first loss — the man who raised her, gone in a single night she hadn't been able to prevent no matter how fast she'd moved. For Vi, that loss wasn't tangled with guilt the way it was for Powder. It was closer to failure: the one person she'd been trained to protect, and she hadn't been there in time, despite everything he'd built her to be capable of.

The second loss took longer to even register. Locked away with no way to know what had happened to Powder, Vi carried both absences at once — one she understood completely, and one she wouldn't understand at all until she got out and started looking. She'd been raised to endure danger, responsibility, even loss. Nothing had prepared her to endure not knowing what had happened to the other person she'd been raised to protect — a gap she carried out of prison, years later, still unresolved.

Powder & Vander

Family — File Case

Powder & Vander

Taken In

Vander didn't have to take in two more mouths to feed. Zaun didn't reward that kind of softness, and he'd seen enough of what the fight against Piltover cost to know it better than most — the version of him that ran the Last Drop had learned that lesson the hard way, not decided it lightly. But when Vi and Powder had nowhere else to go, he made room for them anyway, alongside Mylo and Claggor, and never once made them feel like they owed him for it.

For Powder specifically, being taken in meant something a little different than it did for Vi. Vi was old enough to understand what was actually happening — that she and her sister had been given somewhere to land, and that Vander was choosing, deliberately, to give it to them. Powder was younger, small enough that his house wasn't an arrangement she was old enough to recognize as one. It was simply where she was from.

He never made her feel like her place there was conditional, either — not something she had to keep earning, not a kindness that could run out. Taking in children who weren't his own wasn't a departure from the man Vander had become after turning away from violence — it was consistent with him, one more way of building something instead of tearing it down. Powder never had reason to doubt she belonged in the middle of it.

The Patient One

What Powder needed most wasn't protection from consequences — it was someone who wouldn't treat her as a problem to be managed. Her tinkering was the clearest place that showed up: sparks, smoke, the occasional small disaster whenever she took something apart and didn't quite get it back together right. Mylo and Claggor treated it as something to mock her for. Vander didn't.

His rules about safety and about what the household could afford to lose still applied to her the same as anyone — his patience was never the same thing as no boundaries at all. But where the others saw a mess waiting to happen, he saw a kid figuring out how things worked, and made room for that instead of shutting it down. It wasn't the tinkering itself he was patient with. It was her.

That difference mattered more than it might have looked from the outside. Being teased for the same thing Vander quietly accepted taught Powder two versions of how she was seen under one roof — and his was the version she trusted enough to keep building in front of.

The One Exception

Vander's rules existed to keep his family alive, and he expected all of them — Vi, Mylo, Claggor — to answer to what he judged Zaun would and wouldn't let them get away with. Powder was the one his own instincts bent around, even when he never named it as an exception at all.

It showed less in anything he said than in the shape of what he allowed each of them to face. Vi grew up expected to hold her own — to fight, to take a hit, to be tough enough that Vander could rely on her the way he relied on Mylo and Claggor. Powder never carried that same expectation. She was younger, smaller, still enough of a child that Vander's protectiveness didn't ease as she grew the way it eased with the others; if anything, the gap between how he treated her and how he treated Vi only became more visible as Vi got older and Powder, in his eyes, seemingly didn't. It wasn't that he thought less of what Powder could do. It was that some part of him kept measuring her against how young she'd been when she first came into his house, long after the rest of the household had stopped measuring her that way.

Vi noticed the difference and resented it more than once, certain it meant Vander saw Powder as something to shield and her as something to toughen. Powder rarely registered it as a double standard at all. To her, it simply looked like being cared for — which, in its way, it was.

The Last Night

The job that was supposed to be like every other job wasn't. Powder's device went off when it shouldn't have, and Vander was killed in the chaos that followed — caught between the fallout of her mistake and the enforcers it brought down on all of them. There was no last conversation, no chance for either of them to make sense of what had happened before it was over.

For Vi, that night meant losing the man who raised her. For Powder, it meant something heavier: losing him in a way she couldn't separate from her own hands on the device that triggered it. The two losses look the same from the outside. They didn't feel the same from where she was standing.

Everything Vander had spent years building around her — the patience, the instinct to shield her first, the sense that she was safe simply by being his — ended in the same instant, and by the same cause. What replaced it wasn't just grief. It was grief with nowhere to put itself except on her.

What She Carries

Grief alone would have been hard enough. What Powder carries instead is grief tangled up with blame she reaches for on her own, well before anyone else has the chance to talk her out of it — the device was hers, the mistake was hers, and with Vi gone in the same night, there's no one left who might have told her otherwise before the belief had already settled in.

That's the child Silco finds when he takes her in — not someone he's already working to break, but someone already broken in a specific, exploitable way. Their relationship grows from there, not from a plan he arrives with. It's only over time, as he shapes what she becomes, that the guilt she walked in with stops being something she carries and starts being something closer to who she is. He didn't have to convince her she was responsible. He only had to decide what to do, eventually, with a kid who already believed it.

Jinx & Silco

Jinx & Silco

The One Who Stayed

Powder didn't come to Silco whole and get broken by him. She arrived already carrying the belief that she'd destroyed everything — Vander, the only home she'd known, the sister who'd been taken before either of them could say a word to each other. Silco didn't put that guilt there. He simply didn't leave once he found her holding it.

That was the whole of what drew her to him, at first — not ideology, not manipulation, just the fact that everyone else who mattered was gone and he wasn't. He recognized something in her he'd lived himself: what it costs to be betrayed and discarded by people you trusted. Two damaged people finding something familiar in each other isn't the same as one shaping the other from nothing. That part came later. This part was just Silco staying when staying was the only thing that mattered.

Like a Daughter

What grew between them wasn't a leader collecting a useful weapon. Silco raised her — taught her, protected her, trusted her with more than he trusted most of the people who'd been loyal to him for years. The things that got her mocked or feared elsewhere, the inventing, the instability, the sharp edges other people flinched from, he found a place for instead of a cure.

None of that erases what else he was. His love for her existed in the same man capable of the violence and control that ran Zaun's underworld, and he never separated raising her from shaping her to see the world the way he did. That's not a contradiction he resolved. It's just what the relationship actually was.

And it went both ways. Jinx's loyalty to him wasn't fear dressed up as devotion — she loved him, plainly, the way a kid loves the one adult who never once treated leaving as an option. Silco gave her the thing Vander's death had taken: certainty that someone would stay. That certainty is what she was actually loyal to.

Made Jinx

No single person made Jinx. Vander's death made her. Vi's disappearance made her. Years of guilt, trauma, and a city that gives kids very few soft places to land made her. Silco's role wasn't authorship — it was reinforcement. He didn't invent the belief that trust gets you hurt or that weakness gets exploited. He confirmed it, gave it a name, and gave the girl who already believed it a place to become someone built around that belief instead of buried under it.

The name itself mattered more than it might look like from outside. Being Jinx wasn't just a rebrand — it was Silco handing her an identity that came with acceptance attached, on the condition that she kept being exactly the person that identity described. Powder had been someone who might be forgiven. Jinx was someone Silco had already decided didn't need forgiving, as long as she stayed his.

That's why his approval mattered so much more than power alone could explain. She wasn't chasing his authority. She was chasing the same reassurance every version of this relationship had been built on from the first day: that she was still his, and that this time, nobody was leaving. Silco didn't build that need in her out of nothing. He answered a need that already existed — and in answering it, over and over, he became inseparable from who she turned into.

The Fear of Losing Her

Vi's return didn't just threaten Silco's plans — it threatened the one thing he'd built his entire relationship with Jinx around: the certainty that he wouldn't be left. He understood exactly what he was looking at, because he'd never stopped watching for it. Jinx still loved her sister. That love hadn't been erased by anything he'd given her, only buried under it.

So he did what someone who knows abandonment intimately does when it comes near again: he tightened his grip instead of loosening it. His protectiveness curdled into control, and Jinx's own paranoia and instability gave him plenty of ground to justify it on. Not because he stopped loving her — because loving her had always meant, to him, keeping her close enough that leaving wasn't an option. The question underneath all of it was never simple. It wasn't just which person Jinx would choose. It was which version of herself she could survive being, without losing the one person who'd never once let go.

The Choice

Everything came down to the thing Silco had spent years bracing against: the daughter he'd saved, choosing the sister who'd left her. He'd built a life on the belief that love and survival couldn't coexist — that staying safe meant staying in control, and control meant never letting the people you loved get close enough to leave.

In the end, faced with losing her anyway, he chose her over the goal he'd spent his life pursuing. That doesn't undo what he did to get there, or make his death a redemption — he stays responsible for everything that came before this moment, the damage as real as the love. Both things are true at once, inconveniently, the way they were the entire time.

What he leaves Jinx with is the same wound in a new shape: someone she loved is gone, and she's the one left standing in it, again. But Silco doesn't disappear with his death the way Vander did. His worldview stays lodged in her — the belief that trust is dangerous, that weakness gets exploited, that being left behind is the one thing that must never happen again. He's gone. What he taught her isn't.

Vander & Silco

Family — File Case

Vander & Silco

Brothers in the Cause

Before either of them was defined by what came after, Vander and Silco stood on the same side of the same fight — not just allies working toward the same outcome, but close enough to call each other brothers. Both wanted Zaun free of Piltover's grip, and for a long while, that shared want was enough to make every other difference between them feel small.

It wasn't nothing, though. Even inside the friendship, the seed of what would split them was already there. Vander carried a growing unease about what the fight cost the people it was supposedly for. Silco stayed fixed on the cause itself, willing to let the cost climb if the cause demanded it. Neither of those instincts had won out yet. They were just two ways of caring about the same future, sitting side by side, not yet forced to choose between each other.

The Divergence

The split didn't arrive as betrayal — it arrived as two honest answers to the same question, pulling further apart the longer both men held their ground. Vander started asking what good freedom was if the people being freed didn't survive to see it. Silco started asking what survival was worth if Zaun never became more than a place to merely endure.

Vander chose stability — protecting the Zaun already in front of him, imperfect and intact, over one burned down in pursuit of something better. Silco read that choice as surrender, a brother stepping back from the fight at the exact moment it mattered most. Neither man changed overnight. They just kept answering the same question differently, until the distance between the answers became the whole relationship.

The Betrayal

Disagreement is survivable between brothers. What happened next wasn't just disagreement — Vander turned against Silco directly, moving to stop him rather than simply part ways with him. However justified it looked from Vander's side, protecting the people caught between them, Silco didn't experience it as politics. He experienced it as the one person who'd stood beside him choosing to end him instead.

The near-drowning that followed became more than an injury. It became the defining fact of everything after — a wound Silco carried in his voice for the rest of his life, permanent proof that the brother who once fought alongside him had, in the end, tried to drown him rather than let him keep fighting.

Neither man walks away from this moment clean. Vander did what he believed necessary to stop a future he thought would cost Zaun too much. Silco survived believing Vander had chosen comfort over him, the cause, and everything they'd once shared. That's the exact point where disagreement stopped being ideological and became personal, permanently.

What Remained

Years later, they lived on opposite sides of a city they'd once tried to save together. Vander built the Last Drop and filled it with children who weren't his — Vi, Powder, Mylo, Claggor. Silco built power of his own and never let go of the dream the two of them used to share, even after everything that had happened between them.

What makes this stretch of their history tragic isn't distance — it's how little distance actually did. Silco understood exactly what Vander had given up when he stepped back from the fight. Vander understood exactly what Silco was still willing to sacrifice to keep it going. Neither of them had to guess at the other's reasoning anymore, because they'd once shared it themselves.

That's what made them dangerous to each other in a way strangers couldn't have been. Enemies who've never known each other can be reduced to threats. Vander and Silco never had that luxury — every move one made, the other could read as clearly as his own reflection, because for a while, it had been.

The Last Stand

Their conflict finally reached the point neither of them could keep deferring. Vander moved to protect the children in front of him and stop Silco from taking the rest of the Undercity. Silco moved to secure the future he still believed Zaun deserved, whatever the immediate cost. It was the same argument they'd been having since the divergence, now with nowhere left to retreat to.

What Silco carried forward from that loss didn't stay contained to grief. It shaped how tightly he held on to what came after — Jinx most of all. He'd already lost one person he loved to a choice he couldn't undo. Everything he did afterward carries the quiet insistence that it wouldn't happen a second time.

Family — File Case

Singed & Orianna

His Daughter

Before Singed became the man most people in Zaun knew only as a chemist, he was Dr. Corin Reveck — and Orianna was his daughter. The distinction matters because the work he eventually became known for wasn't born from some abstract fascination with pushing science past its limits. Whatever else Singed became, the beginning of this story is much simpler: there was a girl he loved, and he could not accept losing her.

What the series shows of their life together is deliberately limited, but the traces it leaves behind make the relationship unmistakably personal. In Singed's hidden room, Orianna is not presented as a specimen or a piece of research. There is a preserved photograph of her, reminders of the life she had before illness, and the remains of the father who spent years trying to find a way past the thing medicine could not defeat. His science eventually became inseparable from that purpose, but the purpose came first: not discovery for its own sake, but his daughter.

The Illness

Orianna's illness changed the question Singed was trying to answer. Before it, his work could remain a pursuit of knowledge — something difficult, dangerous, and sometimes ethically questionable, but still separate from the person he loved most. Once Orianna's life was threatened, that distance disappeared. The problem was no longer theoretical. It had a face, a name, and a place in his own home.

He refused to accept that illness had to end with her death. When ordinary medicine could no longer give him an answer, Singed began looking elsewhere, pursuing methods that took him further from the limits of conventional science. His work became less about understanding what was possible and more about finding something that could keep his daughter alive. The further he went, the harder it became to separate the scientist searching for an answer from the father who simply could not accept the alternative.

Eventually, Singed placed Orianna in a state of suspended preservation, keeping her alive while he continued searching for a way to overcome the illness that had taken everything else from them. She was no longer living the life she had known, but she was not gone either. That uncertainty became the space in which Singed continued his work for years, treating death not as something inevitable, but as a problem he had not yet solved.

The Price of Saving Her

Saving Orianna stopped being a question of whether Singed could find an acceptable treatment and became a question of what he was willing to sacrifice to find any treatment at all. The boundaries that might have mattered when his work was simply research became harder to defend once the alternative was losing his daughter. Knowledge was no longer valuable because it was knowledge. It was valuable because somewhere inside it might be the answer he needed.

That is where the relationship becomes morally complicated. Singed's love for Orianna is genuine, but it does not make the choices that follow harmless. The same devotion that keeps him searching also gives him a reason to justify methods he might otherwise have rejected. Other people can become part of the cost of preserving one life, while Orianna remains the person whose life makes that cost seem worth paying to him.

Singed therefore becomes difficult to separate into two simple versions of himself: the father who loves his daughter and the scientist willing to cross terrible boundaries are the same person. His love does not excuse what he does, but neither can his actions be understood properly without it. The tragedy is precisely that the thing most human about him becomes inseparable from the part of him capable of abandoning ordinary limits in order to keep her alive.

The Transformation

By the time Singed finally found a path toward bringing Orianna back, saving her no longer meant returning the daughter he remembered to the body she had once inhabited. Years of illness and the measures taken to keep her alive had already changed what survival could mean. The goal had shifted from restoring what was lost to preserving the person who remained, even if the form that person returned in was something neither of them could have imagined at the beginning.

The transformation is therefore more than a scientific achievement. It is the final consequence of the choice Singed made years earlier when he refused to accept her death. The research, the experiments, and everything he was willing to become had been moving toward this one possibility: that Orianna could exist again. He succeeded, but not by undoing what happened to her. He found a way forward through it, turning survival into something fundamentally different from simply getting her old life back.

When Orianna finally emerges in her new form, Singed is there behind her, watching rather than celebrating. The moment is strangely quiet for something he spent so long pursuing. There is no speech explaining whether the price was worth it, and no easy answer about how much of the girl he once knew remains unchanged. There is only Orianna alive again, and a father who spent years trying to defeat death finally looking at the person he refused to lose.

Ambessa taught Mel that power was not something a person could afford to misunderstand. In her world, strength protected what weakness could not, and knowing when to act mattered as much as knowing what to say. Mel learned to read people, recognize leverage, and understand the interests hidden beneath polite words. These were not lessons Ambessa delivered as abstract philosophy. They were the habits of survival she had carried for herself and now expected her daughter to carry too.

But Mel did not simply become the person Ambessa was trying to raise. She absorbed the lessons while questioning the conclusions behind them. She understood that influence could be more effective than force, that persuasion could accomplish what violence could not, and that power did not have to announce itself through fear. Mel became formidable precisely because she had learned from Ambessa, even when she rejected parts of what she had been taught. The difference between them became harder to ignore as Mel grew into her own idea of power. Ambessa believed survival required strength that could be seen and feared when necessary. Mel preferred influence that worked quietly, through alliances, persuasion, and an understanding of what people wanted before they realized they were being moved. Neither of them was naive about the world. They simply believed different things about what a person had to become in order to survive it.

Ambessa saw Mel's restraint as a vulnerability that enemies would eventually exploit. Mel saw her mother's willingness to use force as a cycle that created more enemies than it eliminated. Their disagreements therefore went deeper than individual decisions. Every argument carried the same unresolved question beneath it: was strength something you used to control the world around you, or something you possessed so you would not have to let the world control you?

A Mother's Fear

For all the distance between them, Ambessa never stopped seeing Mel as her daughter first. Her concern became especially clear when she believed Mel was in danger. The world Ambessa came from had taught her that enemies rarely announced themselves before striking, and that anyone with influence eventually became a target. She therefore treated threats around Mel as problems that had to be anticipated and controlled before they could reach her.

But protection was never simple between them. Ambessa's instinct was to remove the danger herself, while Mel increasingly wanted the right to decide which dangers were worth facing. The more Ambessa tried to secure her daughter's future through power, the more Mel experienced that protection as another attempt to determine her life for her. Ambessa could see the threats Mel could not, but Mel could also see what her mother's protection was costing.

That is what made Ambessa's fear so difficult to separate from her control. She did not manipulate Mel because she felt nothing for her. She did it because losing her was one of the few things she could not treat as an acceptable cost. Her love made her willing to interfere, and her fear made that interference increasingly difficult for Mel to distinguish from the very danger her mother claimed to be protecting her from.

Mel never had to choose between being her mother's daughter and becoming her own person. The harder choice was deciding which parts of Ambessa's lessons she would carry forward and which she would leave behind. She kept the intelligence, discipline, and ability to understand power that her mother had given her, but she refused to accept that strength had to mean domination or that survival justified every cost.

That distinction is what ultimately separates them. Mel does not defeat Ambessa by becoming stronger in the exact way her mother taught her to be. She challenges the assumption underneath those lessons: that the only reliable protection comes from controlling the people and forces around you. Mel chooses to use power without surrendering herself to it, even when doing so leaves her more exposed than Ambessa would ever consider acceptable.

Once the first breakthrough proved that their idea could work, Jayce and Viktor stopped being two scientists pursuing the same discovery and became partners building it together. Hextech gave Jayce and Viktor more than a successful invention. It gave them a vision of what science could become if it stopped belonging only to people with the money and education to pursue it. They imagined a future where technology could make difficult things easier, connect people to possibilities that had once been unreachable, and improve lives far beyond the laboratory where they had started. For a while, they were working toward the same future, even if each man imagined its shape differently.

Jayce increasingly became the public face of that future, while Viktor remained closer to the work itself. That difference did not initially separate them. If anything, it made their partnership stronger: Jayce could open doors Viktor could never have opened alone, and Viktor could keep the original purpose of their research from being buried beneath Piltover's politics. Their success belonged to both of them, even when the city began attaching Jayce's name to it.

What made the dream meaningful was that neither had started with the intention of becoming powerful for its own sake. They believed they were building something that could change the conditions people lived under. That shared belief was the strongest part of their friendship, and eventually the thing that made their separation so painful. They would not stop wanting progress. They would simply begin disagreeing about what progress was allowed to cost.

The Divide

The friendship began to fracture when Hextech stopped being only a shared discovery and became something each of them believed had to be used differently. Jayce became increasingly concerned with the consequences of what their work could unleash, while Viktor became increasingly unwilling to let fear of those consequences stop progress. The difference was not that one cared about people and the other did not. Both believed they were protecting the future they had once imagined together.

Viktor's illness made that disagreement more personal. He no longer had the luxury of treating scientific progress as something that could wait for everyone to agree on the risks. Jayce saw the danger of allowing Hextech and the Hexcore to develop without limits, especially when Viktor himself was becoming part of the experiment. Viktor, meanwhile, saw Jayce's caution as another form of hesitation standing between him and the possibility of changing what human beings were capable of becoming.

Entwined

By the time Jayce and Viktor stood on opposite sides of what their work had become, the friendship itself could no longer be separated from the consequences of their shared history. Jayce had helped give Viktor access to the Hexcore, Viktor had pushed Hextech further than Jayce believed was safe, and every attempt by one to correct the other's course only pulled them deeper into the same story. Their paths had diverged, but they had never truly become independent of each other.

What remained was not the uncomplicated partnership they had once known. It was the recognition that each had shaped the other in ways neither could simply undo. Viktor had changed what Jayce believed science could accomplish, while Jayce remained one of the few people whose judgment could still reach Viktor when everything else had fallen away. Even their final conflict carried the weight of the friendship that came before it.

In the end, neither man's story can be understood without the other. They began with the same impossible idea, built its future together, and eventually became divided by what that future demanded of them. The friendship did not survive in its original form, but its influence did. They remained entwined not because they chose to remain together, but because some connections become part of who you are long before you realize how difficult they will be to separate.

What connects Ekko and Jinx is not a friendship that simply survived unchanged. Too much happened for that. Jinx and Ekko ultimately become two people shaped by the same childhood and separated by everything that followed it. They do not simply return to being the children they once were, because neither of them can undo what happened. But the connection between them survives precisely because their past was real. Before Jinx and the Firelights, before enemies and opposing sides, there were two children from Zaun who knew each other. That beginning never completely disappeared.

The distance between Jayce and Viktor did not begin with hatred. It began with the same discovery they had once shared, slowly producing different answers to what science was supposed to become.
